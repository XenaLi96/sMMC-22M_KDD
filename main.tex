\begin{abstract}
Spatial transcriptomics creates a compelling test of whether tissue morphology
predicts molecular state, yet existing benchmarks remain limited by scale,
spot-level averaging, and incomplete biological context. We present
\textbf{\dataset}, a multimodal resource whose current manifest contains
28{,}315{,}247 cell-aligned targets across 99 unique samples (100 versioned
records), 25 tissue categories, three cell-line origins, and three platforms.
The release adds 21 in-house DBiC-seq samples with 53{,}989 paired cells after
quality control, and explicitly distinguishes native Xenium/DBiC-seq
measurements from Visium HD profiles derived by assigning 2-\(\mu\)m bins to
segmented cells. We evaluate all 25 release categories with buffered contiguous
spatial holdouts. Image features are the strongest of image, coordinate-only,
and spatial-KNN predictors in 21/25 categories; on 30 native-Xenium samples the
image model reaches gene-macro Pearson 0.324 (sample-bootstrap 95\% CI
0.277--0.368), versus 0.053 for spatial KNN and 0.046 for coordinates.
Marker/HVG and cell-type-stratified pseudobulk analyses confirm recoverable
aggregate biological structure. Pseudo-spot controls show that coarsening hides
cellular heterogeneity in a density-dependent manner, while normal-to-melanoma
skin transfer quantifies a sharp context shift. These results establish
\dataset as a breadth- and provenance-aware benchmark while delimiting, rather
than overstating, evidence for exact cell-wise molecular recovery.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Spatial transcriptomics (ST) technologies measure gene expression while preserving tissue coordinates, making it possible to ask whether histology can recover molecular state in situ. H\&E images provide a high-resolution structural view of the same tissue, capturing cellular morphology, tissue architecture, and microenvironmental organization. The central modeling question is therefore no longer whether histology contains molecular signal in principle, but whether current datasets and benchmarks can measure that signal under realistic biological variation.

Existing histology--omics resources have substantially advanced the field, especially HEST-1K and STimage-1K4M for paired H\&E--ST evaluation~\cite{jaume2025hest,chen2024stimage}. Their remaining limitations are best understood along three axes. First, scale is still limited relative to the diversity of organs, patients, studies, and acquisition conditions encountered in real tissue data. Second, most benchmarks remain spot-based, so each target averages multiple cells and can hide cell-level heterogeneity. Third, most resources provide image and expression pairs without sufficiently rich biological context such as age, disease state, local neighborhood organization, and cell--cell communication. These omissions make strong benchmark numbers difficult to interpret: a model may interpolate within a narrow cohort while failing under a biologically meaningful shift.

We introduce \dataset as a context-aware, cell-aligned resource organized along
three axes: \textbf{scale}, \textbf{resolution}, and \textbf{rich context}. Its
working KDD manifest contains 28{,}315{,}247 aligned targets across 99 unique
samples (100 records), 25 tissue categories, three cell-line origins, and
Xenium, Visium HD, and DBiC-seq. The resource name is retained from the frozen
22M release, while the versioned manifest records all subsequent additions.
Crucially, ``cell-aligned'' is a common data interface, not a claim that every
platform directly measures whole-transcriptome expression in an isolated cell:
Xenium and DBiC-seq provide native cell records, whereas Visium HD targets are
derived by spatial bin aggregation.

We evaluate \textbf{scale and breadth} with all 25 release categories, seven
frozen pathology encoders, and training-size controls. We evaluate
\textbf{resolution} using native-Xenium targets, spatially buffered holdouts,
marker/program calibration, matched pseudo-spots, and a Visium HD assignment
sensitivity audit. We evaluate \textbf{rich context} with same-organ
sample-held-out tests and a normal/melanoma skin shift. This organization
separates positive evidence from important boundaries: four release categories
do not favor image features; large pseudo-spots do not always reduce prediction
accuracy; Visium HD aggregation is sensitive to segmentation support; and
cross-condition transfer remains weak. Full encoder, transfer, BLEEP-adapter,
caption, and sensitivity results---including negative results---appear in the
appendix.

More broadly, \dataset makes three contributions. First, it provides a
versioned, provenance-aware ST resource with public Xenium/Visium HD data and
new in-house DBiC-seq acquisition. Second, it defines a common cell-aligned
interface while keeping native-cell and bin-derived molecular targets explicit.
Third, it supplies spatially leakage-controlled, multi-organ evaluation that
calibrates gene correlation with marker, pseudobulk, pseudo-spot, and context
shift analyses rather than relying on a pooled score alone.

\section{Related Resources and Benchmarks}

\noindent\textbf{Histology--Omics Resources.}
\dataset builds on three strands of prior work. First, spatial profiling evolved from Spatial Transcriptomics~\cite{stahl2016spatial}, in situ sequencing~\cite{ke2013situ}, MERFISH~\cite{moffitt2016high}, Slide-seq~\cite{rodriques2019slideseq}, and multiplexed imaging~\cite{wang2018multiplexed,eng2019transcriptome} to higher-resolution or higher-throughput platforms such as HDST~\cite{vickovic2019hdst}, Slide-seqV2~\cite{stickels2021slideseqv2}, DBiT-seq~\cite{liu2020dbitseq}, Visium~\cite{10xgenomics2019visium}, high-definition commercial systems~\cite{janesick2023high,oliveira2024characterization}, and spatiotemporal profiling~\cite{chen2022spatiotemporal}. Second, spatial analysis and integration tools, including Giotto~\cite{dries2021giotto}, Squidpy~\cite{palla2022squidpy}, SpaGCN~\cite{hu2021spagcn}, BayesSpace~\cite{zhao2021bayesspace}, Tangram~\cite{biancalani2021deep}, cell2location~\cite{kleshchevnikov2022cell2location}, related cell-type transfer methods~\cite{bergenstrahle2020super,moncada2020integrating,andersson2020single,satija2015spatial}, Impeller~\cite{duan2024impeller}, iMiracle~\cite{duan2024imiracle}, Disco~\cite{duan2025disco}, Muse~\cite{duan2025muse}, Seurat integration~\cite{stuart2019comprehensive,hao2021integrated}, Scanpy~\cite{wolf2018scanpy}, the Human Cell Atlas~\cite{regev2017human}, and Tabula-style resources~\cite{jones2022tabula,rozenblatt2020human}, provide the computational and single-cell reference backbone. Third, benchmark resources such as HEST-1K~\cite{jaume2025hest} and STimage-1K4M~\cite{chen2024stimage} connect paired histology--omics data with evaluation; \dataset extends this direction with cell-level alignment, multi-organ coverage, and transfer-aware splits, as summarized in \Cref{tab:resource_comparison_ed}.

\noindent\textbf{Histology-to-Gene Prediction.}
HE2RNA showed bulk transcriptomic prediction from digital pathology~\cite{pronier2020he2rna}, while Hist2ST~\cite{zeng2022spatial}, BLEEP~\cite{xie2023spatially}, iSTAR~\cite{zhang2024inferring}, and THItoGene~\cite{jia2024thitogene} established spot- or region-level histology-to-gene prediction. These methods are supported by a broader pathology modeling stack: weakly supervised WSI and MIL-style learning~\cite{campanella2019clinical,lu2021data,shao2021transmil,ciga2022self,ilse2018attention}, dense nuclear and cellular modeling~\cite{ronneberger2015unet,graham2019hover,horst2024cellvit}, and studies linking morphology with diagnosis or molecular inference~\cite{bejnordi2017diagnostic,wang2016deep,komura2018machine,kather2019deep}. Our benchmark differs by targeting cell-aligned image-to-gene prediction rather than bulk, slide-level, or spot-averaged prediction, under controlled in-domain and transfer splits.

\noindent\textbf{Pathology Foundation Models and Multimodal Learning.}
Large pathology encoders define this benchmark's representation-learning context. Representative models include QuiltNet~\cite{ikezogwo2023quilt1m}, pathology transformers~\cite{wang2022transformer}, robust pathology pretraining~\cite{azizi2023robust}, UNI and UNI2-h~\cite{chen2024uni,mahmoodlab_uni2h}, Phikon~\cite{filiot2024phikon}, GigaPath~\cite{xu2024whole}, Virchow~\cite{vorontsov2023virchow}, Virchow2~\cite{zimmermann2024virchow2}, H-optimus-0~\cite{hoptimus0}, CONCH~\cite{lu2024visual}, PLIP~\cite{huang2023visual}, PathCLIP~\cite{he2024pathclip}, BiomedCLIP~\cite{zhang2023biomedclip}, and PathGen-style corpora~\cite{sun2024pathgen}. They also build on visual corpora and CNN or transformer backbones, including EfficientNetB0/EfficientNet~\cite{deng2009imagenet,lin2014microsoft,he2016deep,koonce2021resnet,simonyan2015very,szegedy2015going,tan2019efficientnet,dosovitskiy2021image,touvron2021training,liu2021swin,liu2022convnet}, self-supervised and masked objectives~\cite{he2020momentum,chen2020simple,grill2020bootstrap,zbontar2021barlow,caron2018deep,caron2020unsupervised,chen2021exploring,caron2021emerging,oquab2023dinov2,he2022masked,baevski2022data2vec}, and multimodal or language foundation models~\cite{radford2021learning,devlin2019bert,raffel2020exploring,achiam2023gpt4,bommasani2021opportunities,koh2021wilds}. For cellular context and out-of-domain evaluation, it also relates to nuclei-level resources such as PanNuke~\cite{gamper2020pannuke} and public cancer pathology cohorts such as TCGA~\cite{weinstein2013cancer}. Separately, the main evaluation uses two public 10x Visium HD human lung cancer examples as A/B within-assay sources~\cite{tenx_visium_hd_postx_lung_ffpe,tenx_visium_hd_lung_fixed_frozen}. Here, these works serve as background: text annotations provide auxiliary context for future multimodal evaluation, not the primary prediction target.

\section{The \dataset Resource}
\subsection{Design Objectives}
\dataset is organized around three design requirements. First, every record has
a cell anchor, but its molecular provenance remains explicit: a native
platform-defined cell for Xenium/DBiC-seq or a segmented footprint with
bin-derived expression for Visium HD. Second, the resource preserves multiscale
histology and spatial context around that anchor. Third, it spans sufficient
tissue, sample, species, and platform diversity to support fixed benchmark
splits rather than only large-scale pretraining. An overview appears in
\Cref{fig:dataset_overview_ed}.

\subsection{Scale, Coverage, and Sources}
The canonical manifest assigns every record to one mutually exclusive provenance
block and records sample/version ID, tissue or cell-line origin, species,
platform, aligned-target count, segmentation source, gene panel, context fields,
and supported benchmark protocols. This removes ambiguity between primary
acquisition, public aggregation, and processed derivatives.

\paragraph{Scale.}
The current working manifest contains 28{,}315{,}247 aligned targets from 99
unique samples (100 records), 25 tissue categories, and three cell-line origins.
One versioned breast slide explains the difference between samples and records.
Of these targets, 53{,}989 (0.1907\%) are new in-house primary data and
28{,}261{,}258 (99.8093\%) are aggregated public data. The value of the release
therefore lies primarily in harmonization, cell alignment, provenance, breadth,
and benchmark standardization; we do not present the public component as newly
measured biology.

\paragraph{Resolution.}
Xenium targets use native per-cell transcript assignments and DBiC-seq directly
pairs imaging with expression at its cellular grid units. Visium HD begins from
native 2-\(\mu\)m bins; registered bin counts are assigned to segmented cell
footprints and then aggregated. We call the latter \emph{derived cell-aligned
targets}, not measured single-cell RNA. This distinction controls which claims
and evaluation protocols each platform can support.

\paragraph{Rich modality and context information.}
Each cell anchor links local and contextual histology crops, expression, spatial
coordinates, and structured sample metadata; neighborhood, communication, and
metadata-grounded text are optional processed derivatives. Direct measurements,
harmonized fields, and generated artifacts are identified separately in the
schema (\Cref{tab:data_schema}). Text is not used as ground truth in the primary
benchmark.

\subsection{Cell-Aligned Multimodal Structure}

Each record in \dataset is defined around a single segmented or platform-defined cell, rather than around a spot, tile, or slide region. The record links multiscale histology crops centered on that cell with a cell-level molecular target, spatial coordinates, sample and platform metadata, and optional neighborhood or cell--cell communication descriptors. This shared cellular anchor is the key interface that allows image, omics, and context features to be evaluated at the same biological unit.

This design distinguishes \dataset from spot-level histology--omics resources. Instead of asking whether a model can recover expression averaged over a mixed local region, the benchmark asks whether morphology and local tissue context are sufficient to predict fine-grained molecular variation for individual cells. The same cell-aligned schema also provides the input--target format used by STBoost to lift spot-level image-to-gene pipelines into the single-cell setting.

\subsection{Alignment Pipeline}
The alignment process is auditable end to end. We first localize the assayed tissue region, then define cell locations through segmentation or platform masks, and finally extract matched image context and molecular measurements; because errors can compound across stages, each benchmark stage is tracked explicitly. For Visium HD, we convert dense expression bins into cell-level profiles by assigning bins to segmented H\&E cell footprints and aggregating counts with boundary-aware quality control, whereas for Xenium, platform-provided cell boundaries and transcript coordinates already define the aligned cell unit. Each benchmark record therefore contains slide provenance, a segmented or platform-defined cell footprint, aligned molecular counts, and multiscale histology centered on the same cell; \Cref{fig:bin2cell,tab:data_schema} provide a step-by-step view of this construction and the resulting release artifacts.

\section{Benchmark Design and Main Results}
\subsection{Spatially Buffered Breadth Across 25 Categories}
\label{sec:main_breadth}
For the primary benchmark, genes are selected using the training partition only.
Each sample uses four contiguous edge holdouts (\(x\)-low, \(x\)-high,
\(y\)-low, and \(y\)-high) and removes a 5\% coordinate-span buffer between
train and test cells. The image predictor uses frozen CONCH features and a Ridge
head; controls use coordinates alone, spatial \(k\)-nearest neighbors, or the
training-set mean. We use at most 12{,}000 spatially sampled cells per
native-Xenium sample and treat the biological sample---not the cell---as the
macro and uncertainty unit.

\Cref{tab:main_25_category_breadth} reports all 25 release categories rather
than a cell-weighted pooled subset. Image features have the highest
Gene-Pearson value in 21/25 categories. The four exceptions---Bone, Liver,
Plant, and Seed---remain visible. The final row is a separate 30-sample
native-Xenium macro: image Gene Pearson is 0.324 (10{,}000-replicate
sample-bootstrap 95\% CI 0.277--0.368), compared with 0.053 for spatial KNN,
0.046 for coordinates, and approximately zero for the training mean. Paired
sample-level tests favor images over all three controls after Holm correction
(\(p\leq1.9\times10^{-8}\)). The lower top-200-HVG value (0.202) shows that
the task becomes harder as weaker targets enter the panel.

\subsection{Scale and Biological Calibration}
\label{sec:main_scale_biology}
On native-Xenium HHDX011, we evaluate seven frozen encoders with the same
top-50-HVG task and buffered holdouts while increasing the training fraction
from 5\% to 100\%. Every encoder improves monotonically; the encoder mean rises
from 0.177 to 0.205, 0.241, and 0.278, while H-Optimus-0 rises from 0.205 to
0.307. This is evidence that more training cells help within this controlled
range, not a universal model/data scaling law. The full encoder-by-ratio table
is in \Cref{tab:rev_encoder_scale}.

Low gene-wise correlations require biological calibration.
\Cref{tab:main_biological_calibration} therefore adds marker/HVG and
cell-type-stratified program readouts. On the training-selected marker/HVG

\label{sec:main_resolution}

\section{Release, Reproducibility, and Responsible Use}
\label{sec:release_reproducibility}

We release \dataset Bench as an auditable benchmark package rather than only as
a descriptive dataset. The anonymous release at
\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/sMMC-22M-DB75} contains the compact
review subset, canonical manifest, fixed spatial splits, configurations,
environment files, and evaluation scripts, including the updated 25-category
benchmark. These artifacts are summarized in
\Cref{tab:S5_review_artifact_checklist}. The full processed release is larger
than the review subset, but both use the same schema, loader, split format, and
metric definitions.

The release separates the full processed resource from the lightweight review subset, but both artifacts are governed by the same manifest and schema. This lets reviewers verify the benchmark interface on a downloadable subset while still auditing the full scale and source composition through the manifest. The repository also includes license notes, responsible-use terms, and split definitions so that future models can be evaluated without redefining preprocessing or held-out partitions.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The strongest evidence in this study is spatially held-out morphology--gene
correspondence on native-Xenium targets across many tissues. It is not evidence
that histology uniquely identifies every molecule in every cell. Marker/HVG and
pseudobulk calibration preserve aggregate biological structure, but the
cell-type annotations are expression-derived and therefore not an independent
ontology benchmark. Four release categories do not favor images, and the
top-200-HVG result is markedly lower than the top-50 result.

Target provenance limits cross-platform interpretation. Visium HD profiles are
derived from segmentation and 2-\(\mu\)m bin assignment, and the audit shows
substantial support and boundary sensitivity in two of three examples. These
records are useful for stress testing but should not be pooled with native
Xenium/DBiC-seq targets as equivalent ground truth. Pseudo-spots likewise show
that aggregation effects depend on tissue density rather than producing a
universal cell-over-spot accuracy advantage.

Context coverage is incomplete and uneven across public studies. The skin
experiment quantifies condition shift, while the ovarian age analysis remains
sample/age-confounded because age is nested within samples and panels differ.
Incomplete donor identifiers also mean that our new leave-one-sample-out
experiments cannot be called patient-held-out. Captions are label-conditioned,
metadata-grounded derivatives and are not independent biological validation.
These limitations motivate reporting average, worst-context, and support
statistics rather than a single pooled leaderboard score.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented \dataset as a provenance-aware, cell-aligned ST resource with new
in-house DBiC-seq data and an expanded 25-category benchmark. Buffered spatial
holdouts show reproducible morphology--gene signal across native-Xenium samples,
while marker, pseudobulk, pseudo-spot, bin-assignment, and context-shift
analyses define where that signal is biologically useful and where it fails.
The resulting benchmark is designed to make scale, resolution, provenance, and
context explicit rather than to imply that cell-wise molecular prediction is
solved.

\subsection{Xenium Data Source}
\label{sec:comp_path}
\noindent
The \textbf{Xenium} dataset consists of high-resolution spatial transcriptomic data from multiple human and mouse tissues,
covering organs such as lung, pancreas, skin, liver, kidney, brain, and breast.
The data is generated using the Xenium in situ platform, capturing gene expression at subcellular resolution.
The representative frozen inventory below includes 28 collections; the
canonical expanded manifest is maintained separately and includes additional
records.

For the benchmark, Xenium contributes the broader study diversity and the cross-platform evaluation targets. We currently curate 28 Xenium collections spanning human and mouse tissues, with panel sizes ranging from targeted 541-gene panels to larger 5K/custom panels with more than 10k genes. Platform-provided cell coordinates and segmentation masks are used whenever available to anchor cell-level crops in the histology frame. Because the Xenium panels are not uniform, cross-platform evaluation is defined on the \emph{intersection} of harmonized gene symbols between the train and test domains rather than on the union of all genes. This shared-gene protocol is what yields the 465-gene HLCD033 benchmark and the 370-gene HLCP018 benchmark reported later in the appendix.

\subsection{Visium HD Data Source}
\label{sec:st}
\noindent
The \textbf{Visium HD} dataset provides spatially resolved transcriptomic data from multiple tissue types,
including kidney, embryo, breast cancer, lung cancer, pancreas, and brain.
Unlike Xenium, Visium HD offers a higher-throughput approach for large-scale tissue analysis.
\\

\noindent
Together, the Xenium and Visium HD datasets form a comprehensive foundation for cell-level benchmarking, enabling systematic evaluation of foundation models for spatial transcriptomics across organs, species, and imaging resolutions.

For Visium HD we start from the native 2-\(\mu\)m bin grid and the
full-resolution histology supplied by the platform. Bins are registered into
the slide coordinate system using official spatial transforms, after which
segmentation defines candidate cell footprints in the same frame. Expression is
obtained by aggregating counts from intersecting bins; conflicting or
unsupported footprints are resolved or removed deterministically before patch
extraction. The resulting profiles are \emph{derived cell-aligned targets}.

Platform harmonization is handled explicitly at evaluation time. Within-platform Visium HD experiments use the full 18,085-gene panel. Cross-platform evaluation is restricted to genes shared after gene-symbol harmonization between the train and test datasets, and the cached Xenium$\leftrightarrow$Visium runs additionally use shared-gene normalization before retrieval. In the completed appendix experiments this yields 465 shared genes for K$\!\rightarrow\!$HLCD033 and 370 shared genes for K$\!\rightarrow\!$HLCP018 and HLCP018$\!\rightarrow\!$D.

\subsection{Completed quality audits for Visium HD and Xenium}
Before treating the release as a benchmark rather than a loose data collection, we audited both source platforms for the specific failure modes that would most directly invalidate downstream evaluation. For Visium HD, the critical checks are whether aligned cells remain stably anchored to in-tissue barcodes and whether large context crops frequently fall off the tissue boundary. For Xenium, the critical checks are whether cell centroids remain consistent with polygon-derived boundaries when those boundaries are available, whether crop extraction stays within the morphology frame, and whether matching-organ gene overlap with HD is large enough to support fair shared-gene evaluation.

Table~\ref{tab:platform_qc_summary} summarizes the completed audits. The HD audit covers 24 complete samples across 16 organ groups and 10M aligned cells. Organ-level median assignment distance stays in a narrow 10.81--11.89 pixel range, and the mean 224-pixel crop edge rate is effectively zero ($2.95\times10^{-6}$). The Xenium audit covers 42 complete samples across 15 organ groups and 12M detected cells. For samples with polygon support, median centroid inconsistency stays between 0.12 and 0.14 pixels, while the mean 224-pixel crop edge rate remains low at 0.00541. For HD, the weighted top-200 gene fraction ranges from 0.154 to 0.691 across organs, which indicates organ-dependent concentration differences but not an obvious collapse of molecular support.

\subsection{Template-guided perturbation-like niche mining}
\label{sec:appendix_perturbation}
Beyond the benchmark tasks reported in the main paper, \dataset also supports exploratory analyses that combine cell-level coordinates, neighborhood structure, and expression profiles. One example is the search for \emph{perturbation-like} local niches: cells whose local context resembles the direct-plus-neighbor response patterns observed in real spatial perturbation assays. To study this conservatively, we do not treat these cells as true perturbation labels. Instead, we use a template-guided mining procedure derived from Spatial Perturb-Seq to identify candidate regions whose joint local structure is unusually similar to perturbation-like spatial responses.

The procedure has five steps. We first extract isolated single-guide seed cells and matched controls from Spatial Perturb-Seq sample2. For each guide, we summarize the local response pattern using five trend features: a direct-effect score, response strength in the $0$--$8$, $8$--$32$, and $32$--$64$ neighbor shells, and a near-to-far decay statistic. We then build guide-specific response templates from the strongest neighbor-propagating guides and score every cell in a representative Visium HD lung section against these templates. A cell is called \emph{perturbation-like} only if it satisfies adaptive high-quantile thresholds for template similarity, direct strength, near-shell support, and positive spatial decay. On this representative example, the procedure identifies 222 perturbation-like anchors, which are spatially coherent and visibly distinct from random normal anchors (Figure~\ref{fig:appendix_perturbation_like_niches}B).

These anchors exhibit two properties that make them informative as a dataset capability analysis. In HD/k, perturbation-like anchors have substantially higher template similarity to the Spatial Perturb-Seq source templates than random normal anchors (mean cosine $0.969$ vs.\ $0.496$), are embedded in neighborhoods with substantially more active neighbors (mean fraction $0.153$ vs.\ $0.035$), and lie significantly closer to other perturbation-like anchors (median distance $274$ vs.\ $879$ pixels; all $p<10^{-12}$; Figure~\ref{fig:appendix_perturbation_like_niches}C). The same local-neighborhood logic also appears in an independent pseudo-perturbation case study: for the hypoxia-like cell \texttt{jgbiifkn-1}, 6 of its 8 nearest neighbors are also hypoxia-like, the local mean hypoxia score is $0.514$, and the global mean is $0.177$ (Figure~\ref{fig:appendix_perturbation_like_niches}D). These analyses are illustrative rather than causal, but they show that \dataset supports template-guided mining of biologically structured perturbation-like niches.

\section{Supplementary Benchmark and Analysis Results}
\subsection{STBoost framework, architecture, and training objective}
\label{sec:STBoost_appendix_details}
\noindent
This first subsection of Appendix~C introduces our framework rather than a baseline. STBoost is the multi-organ single-cell histology-to-gene framework used throughout the paper: at the framework level, it lifts spot-level image-to-expression pipelines into the single-cell regime by replacing mixed spot crops with aligned cell-centered crops and larger tissue-context crops, and by replacing spot-averaged molecular targets with cell-level expression vectors. At the model level, we instantiate this idea with a routed multi-expert architecture that is explicitly designed for organ-aware prediction, multi-scale image fusion, and multimodal alignment.

\noindent\textbf{Motivation and framework view.}
Existing histology-to-gene models are usually trained either within one organ or within a small number of matched studies, so they do not naturally expose a shared interface for cell-aligned prediction across diverse tissues. Leveraging the 25-category coverage of \dataset, STBoost is designed as a unified framework in which a common prediction interface is preserved across organs while organ-specific experts retain the flexibility to specialize. In this sense, STBoost is not only a single model but also our framework for adapting strong spot-level pipelines to cell-aligned targets under matched preprocessing and leakage-controlled benchmark splits.

\noindent\textbf{Overview.}
As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:appendix_model_overview}, the STBoost system contains 25 organ-specific predictors $\{f_{\theta_o}\}_{o=1}^{25}$ together with a lightweight organ classifier $C(\cdot)$. Given a whole-slide image $I_{\mathrm{WSI}}$ or sample-level histology context, the routing module predicts an organ label $\hat{o}=C(I_{\mathrm{WSI}})$, activates the corresponding expert $f_{\theta_{\hat{o}}}$, and produces a cell-level expression prediction
\begin{equation}
\hat{g}=f_{\theta_{\hat{o}}}(I_c,I_t),
\end{equation}
where $I_c$ is the aligned cell-centered crop and $I_t$ is the larger tissue-context crop extracted at the same coordinate. This routing-and-expert design allows the framework to preserve organ specialization while keeping the image-to-expression interface shared across all completed experiments.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{model.png}
\caption{STBoost framework overview used for the appendix. A lightweight organ router selects an organ-specific expert, while paired cell-level and tissue-context crops are encoded, fused, and aligned to targets for downstream cell-aligned gene-expression prediction.}
\label{fig:appendix_model_overview}
\end{figure}

\noindent\textbf{Hierarchical encoder and fusion.}
Each expert uses a shared multi-scale image encoder $E_v(\cdot)$ together with a residual projection head $P_v(\cdot)$. The cell crop and tissue-context crop are first encoded separately,
\begin{align}
z_c &= E_v(I_c), & z_t &= E_v(I_t), \\
m &= W_2(\mathrm{GELU}(W_1[z_c; z_t])) + W_1[z_c; z_t], \\
h_v &= P_v([z_c; z_t]) = \mathrm{LayerNorm}(m).
\end{align}
This design keeps the local cell morphology and the broader microenvironment visible to the model at the same time. To explicitly couple local and contextual cues, STBoost further applies a cross-attention module that lets tissue-context tokens query the cell-level features:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
Q &= z_t W_Q, \quad K = z_c W_K, \quad V = z_c W_V, \\
\tilde{z}_t &= \mathrm{Softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d}}\right)V, \\
z_{\mathrm{fused}} &= \alpha \tilde{z}_t + (1-\alpha) z_t,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is a learnable gating coefficient. The intuition is that ambiguous local morphology can borrow information from the surrounding tissue organization, while cells with strong intrinsic visual cues can remain dominated by their own aligned crop.

\noindent\textbf{Diffusion-based regularization.}
To keep predictions biologically smoother and less sensitive to high-frequency noise, STBoost uses a denoising-diffusion-style regularizer on the predicted expression output. Let $\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ and let $\hat{g}=f_{\theta_o}(I_c,I_t)$. The diffusion term is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diff}} =
\mathbb{E}_{t,\epsilon}\!
\left[
\left\lVert
\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta\!\left(\sqrt{\alpha_t}\hat{g}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\epsilon,t\right)
\right\rVert_2^2
\right].
\end{equation}
This regularizer encourages manifold consistency between predicted outputs and the smoother structure expected from real biological expression states, reducing overfitting to noise and isolated artifacts.

\noindent\textbf{Training objective and loss design.}
Each expert is trained with a composite loss
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\lambda_1\|\hat{g}-g\|_1 + \lambda_2\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diff}} + \lambda_3\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{align}},
\end{equation}
where $g$ is the target cell-level expression vector. The first term is the direct reconstruction loss, which keeps the model grounded in per-cell prediction accuracy. The alignment term $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{align}}$ is an InfoNCE contrastive loss that encourages paired image-derived and expression-derived embeddings to share a representation space while pushing apart mismatched pairs. The diffusion term provides a second level of regularization by penalizing predictions that leave the smoother biological manifold. Together, these terms balance pointwise reconstruction, multimodal alignment, and biologically motivated regularization.

\noindent\textbf{Inference pipeline.}
For an unseen WSI, the full STBoost pipeline proceeds in three stages: (1) classify the WSI or sample context into an organ domain using $C(\cdot)$; (2) select the routed organ-specific expert $f_{\theta_{\hat{o}}}$; and (3) predict expression vectors for aligned cells using the paired local/context crops at each coordinate. This yields dense cell-level and tissue-level expression maps while keeping the same architecture across organs. By coupling a shared framework with domain-specific experts, STBoost provides a scalable and interpretable reference system for multi-organ histology-to-gene prediction.

\subsection{Benchmark protocol details}
\label{sec:benchmark_protocol}
The legacy appendix reports three benchmark regimes: \textbf{in-domain},
\textbf{cross-sample}, and \textbf{cross-platform}. In-domain evaluation uses a
left--right spatial split within a sample, with the left two thirds used as the
retrieval/reference bank and the right third held out. For representative
Visium HD sample A this cutoff is 15,600.65 in full-resolution coordinates; for
HLCP018 it is 1,933.71. Cross-sample evaluation changes the test sample, e.g.,
A$\!\rightarrow\!$B, but is not called patient-held-out without verified donor
IDs. Cross-platform evaluation also changes acquisition technology and is
restricted to harmonized shared genes.

These legacy retrieval results use shared preprocessing, target definitions,
and evaluation code. The newer four-edge buffered protocol is reported
separately in \Cref{sec:rebuttal_validation}.

\subsection{Prediction targets and metric definitions}
Two target definitions are used. Within-platform Visium HD runs use the full 18,085-gene panel available in the processed HD cell table. Cross-platform runs use only the shared genes between the train and test domains after harmonized symbol matching. In the completed experiments this yields 465 genes for A$\!\rightarrow\!$HLCD033 and 370 genes for A$\!\rightarrow\!$HLCP018 and HLCP018$\!\rightarrow\!$B, while Xenium in-domain evaluation on HLCP018 uses its native 541-gene panel.

We report the same metric bundle for every completed run: nonzero-detection F1, nonzero-rate difference, gene-wise rank Spearman, cell-wise rank Spearman (reported as transposed rank Spearman), matrix distance, energy distance, and RBF-MMD. Gene-wise and cell-wise Spearman are averaged over the evaluation genes/cells respectively. F1 and rate difference summarize sparsity recovery, while the distribution metrics quantify broader mismatch even when sparse support looks reasonable.

\subsection{Pathology encoder benchmark protocol}
The strongest completed frozen-encoder configuration in this revision uses the UNI2-h pathology encoder under a matched retrieval protocol. Each cell is represented by paired local/context crops with sizes 64 and 256 pixels, resized to the encoder input size of 224. The backbone is frozen, features are extracted once, and prediction is done with top-$k$ retrieval using $k=10$. For the Xenium$\leftrightarrow$Visium cached cross-platform experiments, the same retrieval logic is reused on cached features, together with shared-gene normalization on the expression targets. This is the protocol underlying the appendix benchmark analyses.

\subsection{Failure analysis and robustness notes}
The qualitative lesson from the completed runs is that transfer difficulty is not monotonic in platform alone. K$\!\rightarrow\!$HLCD033 produces a usable shared-gene signal, while K$\!\rightarrow\!$HLCP018 collapses on both F1 and rank-based correlation. This asymmetry suggests that study-specific acquisition, tissue state, or panel composition still dominate simple platform labels. The benchmark should therefore be interpreted as a stress test for realistic generalization rather than a leaderboard on a single homogeneous target.


\subsection{Additional downstream analyses and use cases}
Beyond the core single-cell image-to-gene benchmark, \dataset also supports downstream analyses that probe whether molecular predictions preserve coarse cellular organization. \noindent\textbf{Image-to-celltype prediction.}
We run a marker-derived downstream analysis on the representative sample A using 111{,}772 aligned cells, 10 unsupervised clusters, and a restricted 50-gene target subset. \section{Datasheet for \dataset}
\label{sec:cellnet_datasheet}
\noindent
We provide a DataSheet for \textbf{\dataset}, summarizing its objectives, scope, and design choices, along with potential applications and ethical considerations.
The datasheet below emphasizes the benchmark-facing aspects of the release: the unit of analysis is the aligned cell, benchmark tracks are defined through explicit split files, and the released schema is versioned so that the same processed assets can be used for both modeling and evaluation.

\subsection{Motivation for dataset creation}
\textbf{Why was the dataset created?} \\
\dataset is a large-scale cell-aligned dataset featuring paired imaging and
gene-expression targets with multiscale cellular context. Native Xenium and
DBiC-seq records are distinguished from bin-derived Visium HD profiles.
Captioning, cell--cell communication, and attribute annotations are optional
processed derivatives rather than direct measurements or benchmark ground
truth.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Extensive Single-Cell Dataset:} Provides a vast collection of paired image and gene expression data, capturing cellular characteristics at multiple layers and scales.
    \item \textbf{Multimodal Content:} Integrates captioning, cell-cell communication (CCC), attribute annotations, and multi-tier imaging, enabling deeper insights into phenotypic and molecular interrelationships.
    \item \textbf{Benchmark Utility:} Offers a foundational platform for evaluating and enhancing current models, expanding potential applications and analytical perspectives across tissues and modalities.
\end{itemize}
\noindent
\textbf{What tasks could the dataset be used for?} \\
Potential tasks include cross-modal retrieval, cell-type classification, morphological phenotype analysis, spatial domain segmentation, and gene expression prediction from histology images. Users are welcome to devise new applications but are strictly prohibited from attempting to re-identify patients.

\noindent
\textbf{Has the dataset been used for any tasks already?} \\
Yes. The current study demonstrates utility in cross-modal retrieval and few-shot cell typing, among others (\S4). Researchers can refer to the main paper for baselines and performance metrics.

\noindent
\textbf{Who funded the creation of the dataset?} \\
\dataset was developed with institutional support from multiple sources; details are provided in the Acknowledgments section of the main manuscript.

\dataset Bench is intended to provide predefined splits, target definitions, and evaluation scripts for single-cell histology-to-gene prediction under standardized regimes. The benchmark is therefore not only a collection of multimodal data, but also a controlled evaluation package with fixed preprocessing and explicit held-out partitions.

%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Dataset composition}
\noindent
\textbf{What are the instances?} \\
Instances in \dataset are \textit{single-cell} samples, each associated with:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Three hierarchical image patches (cell-level, tissue-level, WSI-level).
    \item Gene expression profiles and structured metadata.
    \item Textual captions describing morphological and contextual details.
    \item Cell-cell communication profiles.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
\textbf{Are relationships between instances made explicit?} \\
Yes. Each cell is uniquely mapped to its corresponding gene expression vector, tissue type, and spatial coordinates. Cell-to-cell communication edges (ligand-receptor pairs) are also provided, capturing local interactions within a 200\,$\mu$m radius.

\noindent
\textbf{What does each instance consist of?} \\
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Imaging data:} Standard TIFF patches (cell/tissue/WSI) at multiple scales.
    \item \textbf{Expression data:} CSV or HDF5 files storing normalized read counts for thousands of genes.
    \item \textbf{Metadata:} JSON or CSV containing cell type, disease state, organ, and additional attributes (e.g., \texttt{Position\_in\_WSI}).
    \item \textbf{Captions:} Automated textual descriptions of morphological features and context.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
\textbf{Is there a label/target associated with instances?} \\
Each cell is linked to a variety of annotations (e.g., \texttt{cell\_type}, \texttt{cell\_disease\_state}). Demographic information is partially available (for certain human samples) and subject to IRB and licensing constraints.

\noindent
\textbf{Are external resources required?} \\
All data are released as part of \dataset; external links to original publications are included in the metadata. Each source dataset is licensed in a manner that permits downstream distribution and derivative works.

\noindent
\textbf{Recommended data splits or evaluation measures?} \\
Yes. We provide recommended splits (train/val/test) for tasks such as gene-expression prediction and morphological classification. These splits are included as CSV files within the repository.

\noindent
\textbf{What experiments were initially run on this dataset?} \\
Our main paper details multiple experiments, including cross-modal retrieval (image$\leftrightarrow$gene) and spatial domain segmentation. Please refer to the Results section for a comprehensive discussion.

Benchmark metadata makes the release directly executable. Each retained cell is associated with study, sample, platform, organ, and spatial-coordinate fields; benchmark tracks record whether the cell belongs to in-domain, cross-sample, or cross-platform evaluation; and review subsets preserve the same schema as the full release even when only lightweight assets are mirrored during anonymous review.

\subsection{Data collection process}
\noindent
\textbf{How was the data collected?} \\
\dataset aggregates single-cell data primarily from 10x Genomics (Xenium \& Visium HD) and in-house experiments. Each study provides histopathology images, transcriptomic profiles, and relevant sample metadata.

\noindent
\textbf{Who was involved?} \\
All authors of this paper contributed to data curation and validation. Original acquisition protocols (e.g., IRB approvals, tissue preparation) are documented in the source publications.

\noindent
\textbf{Over what time frame was the data collected?} \\
The dataset spans public studies published between 2016 and 2025, along with ongoing additions as new data become available.

\noindent
\textbf{Is the dataset a sample from a larger set?} \\
We aim to include all single-cell data from the selected studies. Instances are unique pairings of morphological patches and expression profiles. Minor omissions may arise if crucial alignment files are missing.

\noindent
\textbf{Known errors or sources of noise?} \\
Whole-slide images vary in quality due to staining differences, compression artifacts, and resolution limits. Gene expression data may exhibit batch effects or sequencing noise. Users are encouraged to apply standard normalization/denoising procedures as needed.

Studies were included only when both histology and spatial-molecular coordinates were available and could be aligned at cell level. Samples lacking reliable coordinate transforms, segmentation support, or sufficient image coverage were excluded from benchmark construction. Repeated sections are handled at the sample/split level so that duplicated material does not leak across the realized benchmark partitions.

\noindent
\textbf{What preprocessing was done?} \\
\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Gene expression standardization:} Data stored in uniform formats (Scanpy objects, CSV) with consistent gene labeling.

For the benchmark tracks reported in this appendix, preprocessing is fixed rather than tuned per method. Retrieval experiments use paired 64-pixel and 256-pixel crops resized to 224 for the encoder; Visium HD uses processed cell-aligned expression derived from native 2\,$\mu$m bins; Xenium uses platform cell tables or cached features; and communication summaries use a 200\,$\mu$m neighborhood definition. Shared-gene cross-platform benchmarks are computed from the processed release itself, not from hidden internal files, so the released assets are sufficient to reproduce the reported tables.
